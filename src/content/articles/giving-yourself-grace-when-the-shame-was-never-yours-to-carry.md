I think one of the things I’ve had to focus on for probably as long as I can remember is trying to find any kind of hope in any of the things that happened to me.

A lot of what I went through, really, the majority of what I went through, wasn’t anything I asked for. In fact, I have the benefit of having the long understanding that this was something I didn’t deserve.

Even when I was a kid, I could look at what was happening and know that I hated it. I knew something about it wasn’t right.

But growing up in that kind of environment still affects you.

You grow up in survival.

You grow up with emotional dysregulation.

You grow up developing behaviors that make sense inside the environment you’re trying to survive, even if those behaviors become difficult later.

And one of the hardest realizations for me was eventually understanding that a lot of the character traits I didn’t like about myself were trauma responses.

That really sucked.

Because I thought I was being authentic.

And then you find out that some of the things you thought were just *you* were actually things you learned because you needed them.

That doesn’t necessarily make those parts fake. What I mean is that I had to start separating what naturally belonged to me from what had been built around survival.

But there was one thing that had always been consistent.

I always wanted to grow.

I always wanted to learn.

I always wanted to move forward.

And no matter what, it seemed like I kept getting pulled back into the same things.

That’s one of the things trauma can do.

Life happens to you, and then the effects of what happened keep showing up long after the original situation is gone.

**I Started Asking a Different Question**

I started giving myself grace when I began understanding that the things that came from trauma weren’t things I asked for.

They had names.

Emotional dysregulation was one of them.

And then I started asking myself questions.

Whose responsibility was it to teach me emotional regulation?

The adults around me?

The same adults who couldn’t regulate themselves?

Okay.

Well, then maybe I need to give myself some grace on that too.

Who were my role models?

Who was supposed to teach me?

What did I actually have access to?

I started piecing together these behavioral things because I wanted to understand why I was the way I was.

And almost every step along the way, there was something else I needed to give myself grace for.

Sometimes it was a survival technique.

Sometimes it was a misunderstanding.

Sometimes it was something that just really needed to be held and understood instead of judged.

**What Point Could I Actually Have Changed It?**

That became one of the biggest questions for me:

**At what point could I actually have done something differently?**

Not in hindsight.

Not with the knowledge I have today.

Not as an adult looking backward.

At the actual time it was happening.

What options did I have?

What did I know?

What had I been taught?

What power did I have?

And when I started asking those questions, another question became obvious:

**Who is carrying all the shame?**

Usually, it’s the person who should not be carrying it.

The person who didn’t ask for any of this.

The person who was trying to survive it.

That became one of the clearest contradictions for me: the person with the least control over what happened can end up carrying the most responsibility for it internally.

**My Own Trauma Made This Very Clear**

In my case, a lot of my trauma came from the way I was disciplined.

It wasn’t supposed to be traumatic.

But because of my sensory sensitivity, it was extremely painful.

It was corporal punishment with very strong religious messaging attached to it.

The *Strong-Willed Child* by James Dobson was a book that many parents had around the time I was growing up.

There was a lot of manipulation of religion wrapped into that environment too.

A lot of conditioning around submitting to authority.

A lot of being taught that authority understood God better than you did.

When I started really looking at that, I had to give myself even more grace.

Because the more I understood the environment I grew up in, the more I realized:

**I had almost no shot.**

What was I supposed to do with that as a kid?

Where was the exit?

Where was the alternative model?

Where was the person teaching me something different?

That doesn’t mean I don’t have responsibility for what I do now.

It means responsibility in the present is different from blame for how something originally developed.

That distinction matters.

**You Don’t Need to Completely Understand It Before Giving Yourself Grace**

You can go all the way down the path of understanding.

You can figure out exactly why something happened.

You can understand the nervous system.

You can understand the behavior.

You can understand the trauma response.

You can understand your childhood.

You can eventually understand, throughout your whole being, that you didn’t deserve what happened.

But you don’t have to finish that entire process before you give yourself grace.

Understanding every detail is not a prerequisite.

You already know you didn’t deserve what happened.

And even if emotionally you don’t fully believe that yet, you can still recognize it logically.

Grace can start there.

**Sometimes the Feeling Doesn’t Catch Up Right Away**

One of the things I think is important is recognizing that there can be an emotional barrier.

You can logically know that grace is needed.

You can logically know that grace is deserved.

And emotionally, you might still feel like you don’t deserve it.

Those two things can exist at the same time.

You don’t need to let the feeling make the decision.

If you could take your life to court and put all of the evidence in front of a jury, and they looked at what happened to you, what choices you had, what you knew at the time, what support you had, and what you were trying to survive—

what would they say?

Would they say you deserved all of it?

Or would they say you deserve some grace?

For me, that thought experiment helped separate evidence from the shame response.

Sometimes your emotions are still carrying an old verdict.

That doesn’t mean the verdict is accurate.

**Look at What You Would Say to Someone Else**

This is another thing that became really obvious to me.

If a friend came to you with the exact same story, what would you tell them?

You would probably tell them:

Love yourself.

Please give yourself some grace.

You’ve been through a lot.

You didn’t ask for this.

You would probably understand them.

You would probably have compassion for them.

You might even feel protective of them.

But then when it’s you?

Suddenly the rules change.

Suddenly all the compassion disappears.

Suddenly everything becomes your fault.

That’s worth noticing.

Sometimes the easiest way to recognize distorted shame is to temporarily remove yourself from the story and ask how you would judge the exact same facts if they belonged to someone you cared about.

**Ending a Pattern Deserves Grace Too**

There’s also something else that I think people miss.

If you become aware of generational trauma and you decide that you’re not going to keep passing it down, that matters.

You put your foot down.

You made it visible.

You called it out.

You decided it stops with you.

That is significant.

You took something that had been moving through a family system and said:

No.

This ends here.

Give yourself grace for that too.

Because that is not a small thing.

That is final-boss-level work. World 8, level 4 - Bowser Koopa.

This is not World 1, level 4.

You’re dealing with something people before you may never have been willing or able to confront.

And you did.

**Grace Isn’t About Pretending Nothing Happened**

Giving yourself grace doesn’t mean pretending everything you’ve ever done was okay.

It doesn’t mean removing accountability.

It doesn’t mean ignoring harm.

It means understanding context accurately enough that accountability does not turn into global self-condemnation.

There is a difference between:

**I did something I want to change.**

and

**There is something fundamentally wrong with me.**

That difference is enormous.

A lot of people with trauma have been carrying shame that belongs to circumstances, environments, adults, systems, or survival responses that existed long before they had the ability to understand any of it.

And then they judge themselves for how they adapted.

**You Have Probably Been Your Own Advocate More Than You Realize**

There were probably times when help would have been good.

Comfort would have been good.

Understanding would have been good.

Someone listening would have been good.

Someone explaining what was happening would have been good.

Someone teaching you how to regulate yourself would have been good.

And sometimes none of that was there.

So you kept going.

You became your own advocate in situations where you probably shouldn’t have had to be.

I’m not saying that because you need another person telling you how “strong” you are.

Sometimes hearing *you’re so strong* gets old.

I’m saying it because there are probably a lot of situations you powered through without the support that would have made them easier.

Give yourself grace for that.

**Blanket Grace**

Eventually, I started realizing that I could analyze this forever.

I could find another event.

Another behavior.

Another trauma response.

Another misunderstanding.

Another moment where I should have been helped and wasn’t.

And almost every one of those roads ended in the same place:

**Give yourself some grace.**

At some point, it becomes easier to stop requiring a separate trial for every single thing you’ve ever blamed yourself for.

Maybe there can just be some blanket grace from the top.

Not because nothing matters.

Not because you never need to change.

Not because everything that happened afterward was outside your control.

But because you are a human being who had things happen to you before you had the ability to understand them.

You adapted.

Some of those adaptations helped you survive.

Some eventually stopped helping.

And now you’re trying to understand them.

You’re trying to grow.

You’re trying to learn.

You’re trying to do something different.

That has been the constant for me.

And maybe that constant deserves some grace too.
