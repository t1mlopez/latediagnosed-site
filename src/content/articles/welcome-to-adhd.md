# Welcome to ADHD

So, you found out you have ADHD.

Maybe that explains a lot. Maybe it also created a hundred new questions.

The first thing to know is simple:

**The diagnosis is knowledge.**

You had ADHD before you knew you had ADHD. What changed is that now you have information about yourself that you didn't have before.

That information can help you understand ADHD, understand yourself, recognize what you need, and make more informed decisions about what you do next.

This guide covers some of the things worth knowing as you get started.

<a id="understand-adhd"></a>

## Understand ADHD

<a id="what-adhd-actually-is"></a>

### 1. Understand what ADHD actually is

ADHD is commonly reduced to *“difficulty paying attention,”* but that's an incomplete picture.

ADHD can affect:

- regulating attention
- starting and switching tasks
- working memory
- organization and planning
- impulse control
- activity level and restlessness
- motivation and reward
- time management

And ADHD doesn't necessarily mean you **can't focus**.

You might struggle to concentrate on something you desperately need to finish and then spend hours completely absorbed in something interesting.

Part of understanding ADHD is recognizing that attention can be inconsistent rather than simply absent.

<a id="hyperactivity-in-adults"></a>

#### Hyperactivity can look different in adults

When people hear **hyperactivity**, they often picture a kid who can't sit still.

Adult hyperactivity isn't always that obvious.

It can look like:

- talking quickly or talking a lot
- moving quickly
- constantly changing positions
- bouncing your leg, tapping, fidgeting or pacing
- feeling uncomfortable sitting still
- interrupting or answering before someone finishes
- impatience
- acting or speaking before you've had time to think
- difficulty slowing down
- feeling internally restless even when you appear relatively still

You might naturally **talk fast, think fast and move fast.**

You may also notice yourself speeding up when you're excited, interested, stressed, overstimulated or trying to get a thought out before you lose it.

Not everyone with ADHD experiences hyperactivity. Some people have predominantly inattentive ADHD, while others experience both inattentive and hyperactive/impulsive traits.

The important part isn't labeling every behavior.

It's learning to notice your patterns.

<a id="understand-yourself"></a>

### 2. Understand yourself

Learning about ADHD is one thing.

Learning how ADHD shows up **in you** is another.

Start paying attention.

What helps you start something?

What makes starting harder?

When do you talk or move faster?

What happens when you're interrupted?

What makes you restless?

What kinds of reminders actually work?

Does movement help you concentrate?

What happens when you're tired, hungry, stressed, overstimulated or understimulated?

What environments make thinking easier?

You're building knowledge about yourself.

<a id="connecting-the-dots"></a>

#### Connecting the dots

Late diagnosis gives you information you didn't have when earlier parts of your life happened.

That can cause you to start looking backward.

School.

Work.

Relationships.

Things you struggled with.

Things you were unusually good at.

Coping mechanisms you created without realizing that's what they were.

Things other people criticized.

Things **you criticized yourself for.**

You can look at those experiences again and ask:

**“Does this situation look different now that I see I have ADHD?”**

Sometimes it will.

Sometimes it won't.

ADHD doesn't have to explain everything.

You're looking again with more information.

That can bring relief, grief, anger, validation or confusion. There isn't one required response to being diagnosed later in life.

Give yourself some grace while you figure it out.

You were navigating your life using the information you had at the time.

Now you have more.

## Taking Care of an ADHD Brain

Your brain lives in a body.

Sleep, hydration, nutrition, movement, stress and your overall health can affect attention, cognition and your ability to regulate yourself.

These things aren't ADHD treatments by themselves, but they're worth paying attention to.

<a id="hydration"></a>

### 3. Remember to drink water

This sounds extremely basic:

**Drink water.**

Dehydration can affect concentration, alertness, mood and cognitive performance in anyone. ADHD doesn't mean your body has some special requirement for enormous amounts of water.

The ADHD-related problem can be much more practical:

**remembering to drink it.**

You might become absorbed in something and realize hours later that you haven't had anything to drink.

Don't depend entirely on remembering.

Keep water where you work.

Carry a bottle.

Use reminders if you need them.

Connect drinking water with something you already routinely do.

Some ADHD medications can also cause dry mouth or change your normal eating and drinking routines, so pay attention to changes after starting medication.

<a id="food-and-nutrition"></a>

### 4. Don't forget to eat

Food can present a similar problem.

You get busy.

You aren't paying attention to hunger.

You forget.

Then suddenly it's late afternoon and you've barely eaten.

Some ADHD medications can also decrease appetite.

You don't need a special “ADHD diet.”

You do need adequate nutrition.

If remembering to eat is difficult, try externalizing that too:

- meal reminders
- easy foods you'll actually eat
- keeping food visible and accessible
- preparing things ahead of time
- connecting meals with established routines

If medication substantially affects your appetite or weight, **tell your prescriber.**

Don't silently work around a medication problem your clinician doesn't know exists.

<a id="sleep"></a>

### 5. Pay attention to sleep

Sleep problems are common among adults with ADHD.

Poor sleep can also make attention, memory, emotional regulation and executive functioning harder.

That can become cyclical:

**ADHD can make sleep harder → poor sleep makes functioning harder → functioning requires more effort → stress increases → sleep may become harder.**

Learn your sleep patterns.

If you're consistently having trouble sleeping, talk about it with your healthcare provider instead of automatically assuming it's simply part of ADHD.

If you take medication, also pay attention to whether medication timing appears to affect your sleep.

<a id="movement"></a>

### 6. Move your body

Movement can be particularly useful if you experience hyperactivity or restlessness.

You might discover that movement helps you:

- concentrate
- think
- regulate restlessness
- transition between activities
- tolerate a boring task
- manage excess energy

Research also suggests physical activity may have beneficial effects on some aspects of executive functioning and ADHD symptoms, although it isn't a replacement for established ADHD treatment.

And movement doesn't have to mean going to a gym.

Walk.

Stretch.

Stand.

Pace while thinking.

Take movement breaks.

If sitting still makes something harder, experiment with **not sitting still.**

<a id="treatment-and-professional-support"></a>

## Treatment and Professional Support

<a id="medication"></a>

### 7. Medication: participate in the process

Medication can be extremely helpful for some people with ADHD.

It also isn't one-size-fits-all.

There are stimulant and non-stimulant medications, different formulations, different durations and different doses.

Finding the right medication and dose may take time.

<a id="participating-in-medication-decisions"></a>

#### Be part of that conversation

Learn what medication you're taking.

Ask why it was chosen.

Ask how long it's supposed to last.

Ask what improvements you're looking for.

Ask what side effects you should watch for.

Ask what would indicate that something needs to change.

And consider tracking your experience.

Useful things to track can include:

- ability to start tasks
- ability to stay with tasks
- distractibility
- hyperactivity or restlessness
- impulsivity
- appetite
- sleep
- mood
- when medication seems to start working
- when it seems to wear off
- side effects

Bring that information back to your prescriber.

ADHD medications can affect things such as appetite, sleep, heart rate, blood pressure and weight, which is one reason monitoring matters.

Your role isn't to determine your own prescription.

Your role is to give your prescriber **good information about what you're experiencing** and participate in decisions about your care.

<a id="who-to-go-to-for-what"></a>

### 8. Know who to go to for what

ADHD care can involve several different professionals.

| If you need… | A good starting point may be… |
|---|---|
| ADHD medication | Psychiatrist, physician, psychiatric NP, or another clinician legally authorized to prescribe |
| Medication questions or side effects | Your prescriber or pharmacist |
| ADHD assessment or diagnosis | A professional qualified to assess ADHD, such as a psychologist, psychiatrist, physician, or another appropriately licensed clinician |
| Help managing ADHD in everyday life | ADHD-informed therapist, psychologist, occupational therapist or qualified ADHD coach, depending on the issue |
| Anxiety, depression, relationships or emotional struggles | Therapist, psychologist or another appropriate mental-health professional |
| Sleep or physical symptoms | Physician or appropriate medical specialist |
| Nutrition concerns | Registered dietitian and/or medical clinician |

A therapist can be extremely helpful with ADHD even if they aren't qualified to formally diagnose ADHD or prescribe medication.

Licensing rules differ by profession and location. Don't assume someone's title automatically tells you what they're authorized or trained to do.

It's completely reasonable to ask a provider:

**“What experience do you have working with adults with ADHD?”**

<a id="vitamins-and-supplements"></a>

### 9. Vitamins and supplements: popular doesn't mean proven

You're probably going to encounter supplement recommendations.

Common ones discussed in ADHD communities include:

- omega-3 fatty acids
- magnesium
- vitamin D
- iron
- zinc
- B vitamins

The important distinction is:

**“People with ADHD take this” does not mean “research shows this treats ADHD.”**

Research on nutritional supplements for ADHD is mixed, and the evidence differs substantially depending on the supplement.

Having a nutritional deficiency is also different from taking a supplement because you've heard it's helpful for ADHD.

If you're actually deficient in something, treating that deficiency may be medically important regardless of ADHD.

Supplements can also have side effects, excessive doses can cause problems, and some can interact with medications.

Tell your doctor or pharmacist about **vitamins, supplements and herbal products** you're taking, especially if you're taking prescription medication.

<a id="regulation-overwhelm-and-coping"></a>

## Regulation, Overwhelm and Coping

<a id="learn-your-nervous-system"></a>

### 10. Learn your nervous system

Sometimes the problem isn't simply:

**“I can't concentrate.”**

You're exhausted.

Overstimulated.

Understimulated.

Stressed.

Hungry.

Surrounded by noise.

Being interrupted repeatedly.

Trying to process several demands simultaneously.

Your ability to use executive functions can become harder under those conditions.

That's one reason learning regulation skills can be useful.

Regulation doesn't cure ADHD.

It can help create better conditions for functioning.

Start learning what **overwhelmed** feels like before you've completely reached your limit.

Maybe:

- thinking becomes difficult
- sounds become irritating
- your body speeds up
- your talking gets faster
- you become increasingly restless
- you start jumping between tasks
- decisions become difficult
- everything suddenly feels urgent
- you become unusually emotional
- you shut down

Those are possible signals.

Learn yours.

<a id="build-a-regulation-toolbox"></a>

### 11. Build a regulation toolbox

Different things work for different people.

<a id="reduce-stimulation"></a>

#### Reduce stimulation

- headphones
- quieter environments
- fewer notifications
- dimmer lighting
- stepping away temporarily

<a id="add-useful-stimulation"></a>

#### Add useful stimulation

- music
- movement
- fidgeting
- standing
- walking
- working around other people

<a id="ground-yourself"></a>

#### Ground yourself

- notice what you can see, hear and physically feel
- feel your feet against the floor
- use temperature or another physical sensation
- slow your breathing
- move your body

<a id="reduce-cognitive-load"></a>

#### Reduce cognitive load

- do one thing at a time
- write things down
- remove unnecessary decisions
- temporarily reduce demands

The question isn't whether a strategy looks like the “right” coping skill.

Ask:

**Does this safely help me regulate and function?**

<a id="externalize-memory"></a>

### 12. Stop making your brain remember everything

Externalize things.

Your brain doesn't need to be your calendar, task manager, reminder system and filing cabinet.

Use:

- alarms
- timers
- calendars
- Post-it notes
- visible reminders
- checklists
- recurring notifications
- consistent locations for important objects

Break big tasks into smaller visible steps.

Reduce the number of steps required to start something.

Put things where you'll encounter them.

If you regularly forget something when it's out of sight, **design around that.**

The fancy planner you forget exists isn't better than the ugly Post-it note that gets the job done.

Use what actually works.

<a id="body-doubling"></a>

### 13. Try body doubling

Some people with ADHD find it easier to do something when another person is simply present.

This is commonly called **body doubling**.

You might:

- work beside someone
- clean while somebody else cleans
- join a virtual body-doubling session
- stay on a call with someone while doing an unpleasant task

The other person doesn't necessarily need to help.

Their presence may simply make staying engaged easier.

If it doesn't help you, that's useful information too.

You're learning what works for you.

<a id="burnout"></a>

## Burnout

<a id="old-coping-mechanisms"></a>

### 14. Your old coping mechanisms may stop working

This is particularly important for people diagnosed later in life.

You made it this far somehow.

Look at **how**.

Maybe you relied on:

- urgency
- anxiety
- perfectionism
- overpreparing
- rigid routines
- external structure
- other people reminding you
- working longer
- pushing yourself extremely hard

Those strategies may have helped compensate for ADHD.

Then something changes.

Responsibilities increase.

You become a parent.

Work becomes more demanding.

Structure disappears.

Stress increases.

Your health changes.

Several things happen at once.

And suddenly:

**“The things that used to work don't work anymore.”**

For some people, that loss of functioning or ability to compensate is part of what eventually leads them to seek answers.

<a id="adhd-burnout"></a>

### 15. Understand what people mean by “ADHD burnout”

You'll hear **ADHD burnout** frequently in ADHD communities.

It's useful language for describing many people's lived experience, but it is **not currently a formal medical diagnosis with universally accepted diagnostic criteria.**

The experience people are describing still deserves attention.

Instead of stopping at the label, investigate:

**What demands increased?**

**What capacity decreased?**

**What coping mechanisms stopped working?**

**How much effort was my previous system requiring?**

If you've experienced a substantial or persistent decline in your ability to function, talk with an appropriate healthcare professional.

Depression, sleep disorders, anxiety, medication effects, physical illness and other conditions can overlap with experiences someone might describe as burnout.

## A Few Things to Remember

<a id="adhd-doesnt-explain-everything"></a>

### 16. ADHD doesn't have to explain everything

Once you start learning about ADHD, it's easy to start seeing it everywhere.

Sometimes you'll be right.

Sometimes you won't.

People with ADHD can also experience anxiety, depression, autism, learning disabilities, sleep disorders, trauma, physical health conditions, and ordinary human problems that have nothing to do with ADHD.

ADHD gives you **additional context**.

It doesn't replace every other explanation.

<a id="find-people-who-make-sense-to-you"></a>

### 17. Find people who make sense to you

Community can be incredibly useful after a late diagnosis.

Someone may describe something you've experienced for decades but could never explain.

Suddenly:

**“Wait. You do that too?”**

That recognition can give you language.

It can give you ideas.

It can help you recognize patterns.

And it can make some experiences feel considerably less confusing.

But remember:

**Someone else's experience can give you a question to investigate. It doesn't automatically give you the answer.**

Community gives you lived experience.

Professionals give you professional expertise.

Research gives you evidence.

Those are different kinds of information, and all can be useful for different reasons.

<a id="where-to-start"></a>

## Where Do I Start?

You don't need to completely redesign your life after getting diagnosed.

Start with a few things.

<a id="start-learn"></a>

### Learn

Learn what ADHD actually is—including attention, executive functioning, impulsivity and hyperactivity.

<a id="start-observe"></a>

### Observe

Notice how it shows up **in you**.

When do you focus?

When do you struggle?

When do you speed up?

When do you become overwhelmed?

What seems to help?

<a id="start-look-again"></a>

### Look again

When you're thinking about something from your past or something you've always struggled with, ask:

**“Does this situation look different now that I see I have ADHD?”**

<a id="start-basics"></a>

### Take care of the basics

Drink water.

Eat regularly.

Pay attention to sleep.

Move your body.

<a id="start-external-supports"></a>

### Build external supports

Don't require yourself to remember everything.

Use tools and change your environment when that makes something easier.

<a id="start-regulation"></a>

### Learn regulation

Figure out what overstimulation, understimulation and overwhelm feel like for you—and what helps.

<a id="start-treatment"></a>

### Participate in treatment

Learn about your options.

Ask questions.

Track what happens.

Work closely with your clinicians.

<a id="start-investigate"></a>

### Investigate instead of judging

When something repeatedly doesn't work, try replacing:

**“What's wrong with me?”**

with:

**“What's making this difficult?”**

That's something you can investigate.

---

<a id="final-thoughts"></a>

## Welcome to ADHD

You don't need to understand all of this immediately.

For now, the important thing is that you have information about yourself that you didn't have before.

Learn what ADHD is. Pay attention to how it shows up for you. Notice what makes things easier or harder. Ask questions about treatment. Build systems around the things you struggle with. And when something doesn't make sense, investigate it rather than immediately assuming you're doing something wrong.

Some of what you learn may change how you understand your past. More importantly, it can give you better information about what you need now.

**The diagnosis is knowledge. What you do with that knowledge can come next.**
